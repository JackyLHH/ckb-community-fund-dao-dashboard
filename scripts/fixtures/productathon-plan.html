Summary

Umojaverse is an Africa-focused builder community that brings blockchain, web3 and general tech together from the grassroots and across borders, so people learn and build products of real value. Through campus tours, ideathons, workshops and hackerhouses, it takes people from first curiosity to working prototypes.

The CKB Productathon is a competitive programme in Kenya that turns builders into teams with live products on CKB mainnet. It runs in two parts. The first is virtual: expert-led training on CKB technology and on building a startup, while teams ship and gather real users. The second is a two-day physical hackerhouse on the shore of Lake Victoria, where teams that have gone live build, demo and pitch for prizes. Teams with a working product and teams with a new idea and a credible execution plan can both apply, and the aim is the same: products that ship, win user validation and keep growing.

  • Timeline (tentative): planning in December 2026, programme from January to early February 2027, follow-up support until mid-March 2027.

  • Teams: up to 15 teams of three at the hackerhouse.

  • The rule: only teams live on CKB mainnet can enter the hackerhouse, and to win a prize a team must show on-chain activity from at least 5 beta users.

Why this is needed, why Umojaverse, why now

Why it is needed. Kenya has talented builders and no shortage of events, but too few of them ship products that real users depend on. Workshops and ideathons tend to end at the idea or the demo, and the hard part that follows has little support: deploying to mainnet, finding the first users and keeping the product running.

Three gaps hold builders back. There is no structured path from a first contract to a mainnet product. Mentors who have shipped and found users are scarce and hard to reach outside the big cities. And builders work alone, without a room of peers building beside them to share fixes, pressure and momentum.

CKB makes this gap worth closing. Its cell model, RISC-V based virtual machine and flexible scripts let builders create their own product logic, not just move tokens, but that flexibility is hard to learn alone. Guided, hands-on training turns it into an advantage, and every product that goes live on mainnet with real users is lasting proof of adoption for the ecosystem. Making live mainnet use the measure of success changes the incentive: builders are rewarded for shipping and getting users, not for attending.

Why Umojaverse. Umojaverse is a builder community, not a one-off event organiser, and this programme is the next step of work we already do. We take builders from first curiosity to working prototypes through campus tours, ideathons, workshops and hackerhouses, in person across Kenya and, through the Pulse East Africa HackFest, in five African countries. That gives this programme five things it needs:

  • Reach. An established builder community and presence across Kenya and wider Africa, so the open call draws serious applicants.

  • Proven delivery. Experience running multi-day, in-person hackerhouses and multi-country events, from venues and logistics to mentors, judging and content.

  • Teaching depth. A lead organiser who is a blockchain educator and developer, backed by a network of seasoned builders to teach, mentor and judge.

  • A CKB footing. We are already introducing Kenyan builders to CKB, and we know the questions they ask and where they get stuck.

  • Follow-through. We stay with builders after the event, through follow-up support and public recordings that keep teaching long after the programme ends.

This means we can run the programme end to end: recruit the teams, teach them, bring them together, showcase them and keep supporting them.

Why now. Three things line up. First, the tools are ready: builder tooling such as CCC and OffCKB makes it practical to go from a first script to a mainnet deployment inside a short programme. Second, builder attention in Kenya is warm and will cool without a concrete next step. Recent CKB activity needs a destination with a deadline, prizes and a place to build. Third, the calendar works: planning in December and a January start use the weeks after the holidays, and teams that ship to mainnet by early February get months of real use to prove adoption before mid-year. A month of follow-up support then turns a weekend of building into traction.

Programme format and execution flow

The programme has two parts: a virtual phase, where teams learn and ship to mainnet, and a two-day physical hackerhouse for the teams that make it. The timeline below is tentative and may shift as planning firms up.

  • Plan, open call and selection (December 2026). Curriculum, instructors, venue and partners are confirmed. The open call (14 to 27 December) takes live products and strong plans, and selected teams are announced on Monday 4 January.

  • Virtual phase (9 to 26 January). Starts five days after acceptance. Eight sessions, two a day over four days across three weeks: Saturday 9, Tuesday 12, Tuesday 19 and Tuesday 26 January. Up to 30 teams ship to mainnet.

  • Mainnet submission (27 to 28 January). Two extra days to deploy to mainnet and submit. Beta testers are not required yet.

  • Review and acceptance (29 to 31 January). Two days of review, then hackerhouse teams are announced on Sunday 31 January.

  • Preparation and travel (1 to 5 February). Four days to gather beta users and prepare, with travel on Friday 5 February.

  • Hackerhouse by Lake Victoria (Saturday 6 to Sunday 7 February). Up to 15 teams, with pitches and prizes.

  • Follow-up month (8 February to 12 March). Mentor support, a 30-day live check and a closing showcase.

  • Final report (by Wednesday 24 March).

The programme has three gates:

  • Selection: a live product or a strong execution plan.

  • Mainnet gate (28 January): the product is deployed on CKB mainnet to enter the hackerhouse.

  • Prize gate (7 February): on-chain activity from at least 5 beta users, shown at the demos. Winners are named, and the 30-day check follows.

Applications, selection and the mainnet gate

Who can apply. Teams of up to three builders based in Kenya. The programme does not target existing products only. Live products and new ideas are both accepted, provided a new idea comes with a credible plan to execute. The aim for every team is a product that ships, earns user validation and grows into real adoption.

How applications are scored. Up to 30 teams are admitted to the virtual phase. Applications are scored on execution evidence (30), CKB integration (25), user and problem (20), team (15) and reuse and impact (10). A live product with early users scores well on execution evidence, and a new idea needs a clear build plan and a team able to carry it out.

The mainnet gate and prize rule. To enter the hackerhouse, a team must have deployed its product to CKB mainnet and submitted it by Thursday 28 January. Beta testers are not required at that point. To win a prize, a team must prove on-chain activity from at least 5 distinct beta users who actually use the product, not team members testing themselves. The organisers verify this with transaction links and tester confirmation. If more than 15 teams qualify, places go by application score and the strength of the live product.

Virtual sessions

Eight sessions run two a day over four days. The four startup sessions, on how to build and grow a startup, run from 12:00 to 14:00 EAT, and the four technical CKB sessions run in the evening from 18:00 EAT. Instructors are seasoned builders who have shipped products.

  • Saturday 9 January. Startup session, 12:00 to 14:00: from idea to a validated problem. Technical session, evening: CKB fundamentals, covering cells, scripts and the CKB-VM.

  • Tuesday 12 January. Startup session, 12:00 to 14:00: finding and recruiting your first beta testers. Technical session, evening: building with CCC and OffCKB.

  • Tuesday 19 January. Startup session, 12:00 to 14:00: metrics, feedback and iteration. Technical session, evening: deploying to mainnet, wallets and user experience.

  • Tuesday 26 January. Startup session, 12:00 to 14:00: pitching and demo preparation. Technical session, evening: security and advanced patterns.

Weekly mentor office hours support teams between sessions. Every virtual session is recorded and added to the Umojaverse YouTube channel as a CKB learning resource, and the sessions held during the physical phase are added too.

Hackerhouse, judging and prizes

Up to 15 teams of three gather at a hackerhouse on the shore of Lake Victoria for two days.

  • Day 1, Saturday 6 February. Arrival, kickoff and a full day of building, with mentor rounds and an evening checkpoint.

  • Day 2, Sunday 7 February. Final polish, demos and pitches, judging, results and prizes.

Judging. A panel of at least three judges drawn from experienced builders, the CKB ecosystem and startup practitioners. Teams are scored on live product and real use (30), CKB integration (25), user and problem (20), pitch and team (15) and reuse and impact (10).

Prizes. Four awards totalling $4,000: first place $1,500, second $1,000, third $800 and fourth $700. Only teams with on-chain activity from at least 5 beta users are eligible. Prizes are paid in full once the hackerhouse results are verified.

Team videos. Every team at the hackerhouse gets a neat 3-minute video: 1.5 minutes of short pitch or explainer from the team, then 1.5 minutes showing the product flow, edited into one. The videos are published on X, YouTube and TikTok to win distribution, and show both the founders and the product. We expect about 15 videos.

Follow-up month

From 8 February to 12 March, hackerhouse builders get continued support to keep shipping:

  • Weekly clinics for product, technical and user-growth questions.

  • A mentor session with experienced builders.

  • Introductions to funders and ecosystem programmes.

  • A 30-day live check on Tuesday 9 March to confirm products are still live and in use.

  • A closing X Space on Friday 12 March where teams showcase their progress.
